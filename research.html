Porous Materials

[Porous materials are solids containing potential cavities that allow molecules to enter, be stored, or move through the structure. A wide range of classes, including metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), zeolite, covalent organic frameworks (COFs), porous cage molecules offer different combinations of pore size, surface area, and chemical functionality. Because of their high surface area and tunable pore environments, porous materials are widely studied for applications such as gas storage and separation, catalysis, sensing, and drug delivery. A central theme in the field is understanding how a material's structure — including its pore geometry, connectivity, and any structural flexibility — governs how guest molecules are adsorbed, transported, and released.]

Electron Crystallography

[Electron crystallography is a family of techniques that use electron diffraction, rather than X-ray, to determine the atomic-scale structure of crystalline materials. Since electrons interact much more strongly with matter than X-rays do, electron diffraction can yield structural information from crystals far too small for conventional X-ray crystallography — often a sub-micron sized crystal. Electron diffraction techniques such as continuous-rotation 3D electron diffraction (3DED, also known as microED) have enabled to solve challenging proteins, small organic molecules, and inorganic materials that were previously inaccessible due to insufficient crystal size or quality. This has broadened the range of samples that can be structurally characterized, particularly for materials that resist growth into larger single crystals.]
